We have seen that one of the key questions when trying to create a "successful" data visualisation is to consider what the **purpose** of our data visualisation is. One of the key principles of measuring success is to know what success means. In data visualisation how can we measure how well we have met our aim if our aim is not clear in the first place? This is one of the key things we must keep in mind throughout the visualisation process, and there are several questions we must ask ourselves that can start to clarify this sense of purpose:

- **Why** are we doing this? 
- **What** are we doing this for? 
- **Who** are we doing this for?

At a high level, the answer quite often is going to involve ideas such as 'to communicate', 'to give people ideas', 'to make people think about data' - but it is only by going further than that and identifying precise answers that we can begin to create meaningful and useful visualisations.

## Data Visualisation Principles

Look at the list of the 'Top 10' principles for data visualisation you wrote down in the last section. More than likely, you started with high level principles, like 'Tell the truth', 'Make things clear', 'Keep things simple', and perhaps added in more specific guidelines such as 'Avoid Pie Charts' or 'Make sure axes are always labelled'. 

Many of the high-level principles that we might identify can be examined at multiple levels to consider how they might apply to visualisation, and how they might impact our process and our outputs.

At the same time, we can consider the more specific guidance and rules we have written down and consider not just how well they align with the higher level principles, and when and where they might apply, or more interestingly, when and where it might be safe to ignore them.



### Truth

There are of course many different ideas about what truth means. We have already seen that in data visualisation we are constantly making choices that impact the understanding of data, and so what we understand by truth has an impact throughout the process. Is 'truth' about making sure our visualisation fully represents the data accurately, or is 'truth' about making sure our visualisation presents our stated aim clearly, even if our aim is perhaps misaligned with what the data says? This is something we will return to throughout this module as we think about ethical visualisation, and how we can use visualisation to present data in a fair and balanced way. In terms of practical applications, this will start to bring in thoughts of data distortion and maintaining integrity in visual representation.


### Clarity

Our visualisations should be clear and understandable - having decided what the truth is that we are attempting to communicate, this truth should be easy to grasp for our users and readers. Our visualisation should not work against this aim by hiding or obfuscating, or by deliberately distorting the data representations. However, this though also brings in some of the considerations we have already seen around the balance of aesthetics and minimalism in terms of balancing the desire for a visualisation to be understandable, but also the drive to engage and attract users and readers, and use suitable visual hooks to aid memory and understanding. Complexity is often the enemy of clarity, but we have already seen examples of visualisations that are more complex, and as a result are more memorable and draw further engagement with the reader, so is complexity necessarily always a bad thing?


### Efficiency

When we talk about efficiency in data visualisation terms, we might start to think about the speed in which a reader or user can draw conclusions from the graphic. This clearly relates to the ideas of **clarity**, but also might draw us to start to think about things like the visual hierarchy and the layout of the visualisation, and how we can use our space to communicate most effectively.


### Accessibility

Accessibility is perhaps one of the more practical high level principles that we may consider, and is typically connected to **clarity** and **efficiency** in terms of consideration of properties such as Typography and Visual Hierarchy. Here we may also start to think about things like the use of colour, particularly considering things such as contrast ratios and colour blindness. 


### Intentionality

Again, a high level concept that overlaps somewhat with the idea of '**truth**', in that here we are ensuring that our visualisation and in particular the method that we choose to represent our data fully supports the aims of our visualisation and the nature of the data that we are visualising. 


## Avoiding Rules

Widely cited design directives (e.g., "always start line chart y-axes at zero" or "never use pie charts") are opinions rather than universal laws. Every design choice depends on context, and rules can often be legitimately broken when appropriate.
