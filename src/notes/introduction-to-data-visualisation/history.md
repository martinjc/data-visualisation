## Visualisation History

Data visualisation has a rich history of translating complex information into actionable insights, and many of the techniques we use today were pioneered centuries ago. In this field it is often the case that the principles and techniques remain the same, it is the tools we use to implement them that change.

Early examples of this shows the development of early spatial mapping laying the groundwork for modern techniques. In 1686, Edmond Halley created a highly detailed map of global trade winds, utilizing pen strokes and line proximity to indicate wind direction and intensity:

Now, hundreds of years later, these foundational methods are mirrored in modern live-weather visualizations like [earth.nullschool.net](https://earth.nullschool.net/)

{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/nullschool.gif" "earth.nullschool.net" "[https://earth.nullschool.net/](https://earth.nullschool.net/)"%}

Centuries after Halley, in 1855, Jon Snow famously mapped cholera cases in London, successfully tracing the outbreak to a specific water pump and demonstrating the profound power of visual analysis in solving real-world crises.

{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/jonsnow.jpg" "Jon Snow's Cholera Map" "Snow, J (1855) 'On the Mode of Communication of Cholera'"%}

Another geographical visualisation achievement often considered as foundational is Charles Minard's map of the Napoleonic invasion of Russia, which innovatively combined geographical layouts, army attrition, timelines, and temperature drops into a single, cohesive graphic.

{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/minard.png" "Charles Minard's Map" "Minard, Charles J “Tableaux Graphiques et Cartes Figuratives de M. Minard 1845-1869”. Bibliothèque de L’Ecole Nationale des Ponts et Chausees, Paris, Item 28"%}

Beyond spatial mapping, data visualisation evolved to communicate critical statistics and social conditions. William Playfair is widely credited with inventing and popularizing foundational graphs, such as the line and area charts, which remain ubiquitous in modern analysis. 

{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/playfair.png" "William Playfair Line and Area" "William Playfair's Time Series of Exports and Imports of Denmark and Norway"%}

During the Crimean War, Florence Nightingale used "rose charts" to visually communicate complex health statistics to decision-makers, making the underlying data both accessible and persuasive.

{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/nightingale.jpg" "Florence Nightingale's Rose Charts" %}

Similarly, sociologist W.E.B. Du Bois employed bold, primary-colored visualizations to document the post-slavery conditions of Black Americans, proving that data visualisation is a compelling tool for social commentary. 


{% image "src/img/introduction-to-data-visualisation/dubois.png" "W.E.B. Du Bois Visualisation" "Introductory chart from “A series of statistical charts illustrating the condition of the descendants of former African slaves now in residence in the United States of America”, 1900, via Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division. W. E. B Du Bois"%}


Understanding the history of the field of data visualisation is an important part of becoming an effective visualisatin practitioner. The techniques and challenges faced and solved by the pioneers in the field will often drive our own solutions when we approach visualising data today.
